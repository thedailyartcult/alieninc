# The Taxonomy of Capability — Mapping What Cannot Be Automated

## The Automation Narrative

The dominant narrative about automation in the early twenty-first century was simple and optimistic: machines would take over the repetitive, the routine, and the mechanical, freeing human beings to do what they do best — creative, relational, and strategic work. This narrative was comforting. It implied a clean boundary between what machines could do and what humans could do, and it suggested that the boundary would always favor humans in the domains that mattered most.

The narrative was wrong. Not in its direction — machines have indeed automated enormous amounts of routine work — but in its implicit claim that the boundary between automatable and non-automatable was stable, knowable, or permanent. The boundary has shifted, and it continues to shift, in ways that are difficult to predict and harder to map.

This archive article proposes a taxonomy of capability that attempts to map the terrain more honestly. It is not a prediction about what will be automated next. It is a framework for understanding why some capabilities resist automation, why some capabilities surrender to it unexpectedly, and what the distinction between the two reveals about the nature of capability itself.

## The Four Axes of Capability

We propose that capabilities can be understood along four axes, and that the position of a capability on each axis determines its vulnerability to automation.

**Axis 1: Physical Embodiment.** Capabilities that require physical interaction with the world — manipulating objects, navigating unpredictable environments, performing tasks that require fine motor control or sensory integration — have historically been the last to be automated. This axis is shifting rapidly with advances in robotics and embodied AI, but the fundamental insight remains: the more a capability depends on a physical body operating in a physical world, the more resistant it has been to automation. This resistance is not permanent, but it has been durable, and it is worth understanding why.

**Axis 2: Social Context.** Capabilities that depend on reading and responding to social cues, navigating interpersonal dynamics, or operating within cultural norms have been surprisingly resistant to automation. This is not because social intelligence is inherently difficult — it is because social intelligence is context-dependent in ways that are hard to formalize. A joke that works in one culture fails in another. A gesture that signals respect in one setting signals condescension in another. The social context is not a fixed set of rules; it is a living, shifting, negotiated reality. Machines can approximate social behavior, but they cannot yet inhabit social context the way a human being does.

**Axis 3: Normative Judgment.** Capabilities that involve making judgments about what ought to be done — ethical reasoning, aesthetic evaluation, strategic prioritization — are the most resistant to automation of all. This is not because normative judgment is uniquely human in some mystical sense; it is because normative judgment requires the ability to weigh competing values, to recognize when the framework itself is in question, and to accept responsibility for the consequences of a decision. A machine can optimize within a given framework. It cannot question the framework. It cannot decide that the framework is wrong and choose a different one. And it cannot bear the weight of that choice.

**Axis 4: Creative Synthesis.** Capabilities that involve generating novel connections between disparate ideas, creating new frameworks for understanding, or producing work that has never been seen before — these have been the most celebrated targets of automation, and the most surprisingly resistant. Large language models can produce text that resembles creative synthesis, but they do so by recombining patterns from their training data. They do not have the experience of encountering a genuinely new problem, or the motivation to solve it, or the ability to recognize that the solution itself changes the problem. Creative synthesis, in the strongest sense, requires a mind that is changed by what it creates.

## What the Taxonomy Reveals

The taxonomy reveals a pattern that is often overlooked in the automation debate: the capabilities that are most resistant to automation are not the ones we typically celebrate as "uniquely human." They are the capabilities that are most deeply embedded in the context of human life — physical, social, normative, and creative — and that depend on the kind of embodied, situated, responsible engagement with the world that no current machine can replicate.

This does not mean that automation will not transform these domains. It means that the transformation will look different from what the dominant narrative predicts. The capabilities that survive automation will not be the ones that machines cannot imitate; they will be the ones that humans cannot afford to delegate, because delegating them means delegating the very capacities that make us responsible agents in the world.

---

The taxonomy is a map, not a territory. It will be revised, extended, and challenged as the technology evolves. But the questions it raises — about what we value, what we delegate, and what we refuse to automate — are questions that will only become more urgent as the boundary between human and machine capability continues to shift.
