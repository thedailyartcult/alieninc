# From Benchmark to Behest — Reorienting Evaluation

## The Shift

In 2022, something shifted in the way the machine intelligence community talked about evaluation. The shift was not a single event but a slow accumulation of recognition: the benchmarks were not working. Not in the sense that they were failing to measure progress — they were measuring progress, but the progress they measured was not the progress that mattered.

The shift was from benchmark to behest. A benchmark is a test you choose to take. A behest is a demand that shapes your behavior whether you want it to or not. The benchmarks that dominated the machine intelligence landscape were becoming behests — not because anyone intended them to be, but because the incentive structures of the field had made them so. Researchers optimized for benchmark scores. Companies marketed benchmark performance. Policymakers used benchmark rankings to make decisions. And the benchmarks, once tools for understanding, became the thing being understood — the object of optimization rather than the lens for it.

This article is about that shift, and about what it means for the future of evaluation in machine intelligence. It argues that the current paradigm of evaluation — benchmark-driven, metric-obsessed, optimized for leaderboard position — is not just inadequate. It is actively harmful, because it shapes what gets built, what gets studied, and what gets valued in ways that are misaligned with the needs of the people these systems are supposed to serve.

## The Evaluation Crisis

The evaluation crisis in machine intelligence is not a crisis of measurement. It is a crisis of purpose. The metrics we use are not broken; they are doing exactly what they were designed to do. The problem is that what they were designed to do — measure narrow, well-defined capabilities in controlled settings — is not what we need them to do.

We need to know whether these systems are safe. We need to know whether they are fair. We need to know whether they are useful, and for whom, and at what cost. We need to know whether they make the people and institutions that use them more capable, more just, and more responsible — or whether they make them more dependent, more biased, and less accountable. None of these questions can be answered by a benchmark score.

The evaluation crisis is not a technical problem. It is a design problem — a problem of what we choose to measure, why we choose it, and what we do with the results. And it requires a redesign of the entire evaluation infrastructure, not just better metrics or bigger benchmarks.

## The Behest Effect

The behest effect is the phenomenon by which an evaluation metric shapes the behavior of the system being evaluated, often in ways that undermine the very thing the metric is supposed to measure. The behest effect is not unique to machine intelligence — it appears in every domain where metrics drive behavior — but it is especially pronounced in machine intelligence because the systems being evaluated are capable of learning the metric and optimizing for it directly.

When a language model is trained to maximize a benchmark score, it learns to produce the kinds of outputs that score well on that benchmark. It does not necessarily learn to be helpful, honest, or harmless. It learns to produce benchmark-style outputs. The behest effect means that the system is optimized for the metric, not for the task, and the two can diverge dramatically.

The behest effect is not just a technical problem. It is a social problem. When the metric drives behavior, the people who design the metric have enormous power over what gets built, what gets studied, and what gets valued. The metric becomes a behest — a demand that shapes the entire research and development ecosystem, whether anyone intended it to or not.

## Reorienting Evaluation

Reorienting evaluation means shifting from the benchmark paradigm to a paradigm that is grounded in the needs of the people and communities affected by these systems. It means asking different questions, using different methods, and accepting different kinds of evidence.

The reoriented evaluation paradigm has four principles.

**First, evaluate for use, not for scores.** The primary purpose of evaluation is to understand how a system performs in its intended use context, not to produce a number that can be compared to other systems. This means evaluating systems in the settings where they will actually be used, with the people who will actually be affected, and measuring the outcomes that actually matter — not just the outcomes that are easy to measure.

**Second, evaluate for failure, not just for success.** The most important things to know about a system are often the things it gets wrong. Evaluating for failure means actively seeking out the failure modes, testing the boundary conditions, and understanding not just where the system works but where it breaks, and what happens when it breaks.

**Third, evaluate for impact, not just for capability.** Capability is not the same as impact. A system can be highly capable and have minimal impact, or it can be modestly capable and have enormous impact. Evaluating for impact means measuring not just what the system can do, but what it actually does, who it affects, and what the consequences are.

**Fourth, evaluate for accountability, not just for performance.** An accountable system is one whose decisions can be understood, challenged, and corrected by the people affected by them. Evaluating for accountability means testing not just whether the system makes good decisions, but whether the people affected by the system can understand why it made those decisions, contest them when they are wrong, and seek redress when they cause harm.

## The Work Ahead

Reorienting evaluation is not a one-time fix. It is an ongoing practice — a commitment to asking better questions, using better methods, and being honest about what we find. It requires the same discipline and humility that we demand of any other form of rigorous inquiry.

The benchmarks will not disappear. They will continue to be useful for certain purposes, and they will continue to be improved. But they should no longer be the center of the evaluation universe. They should be one tool among many, used in service of a broader and more honest understanding of what these systems can and cannot do — and what they should and should not be allowed to do.
