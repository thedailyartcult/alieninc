# The Smil Problem — Notes Toward a Correct Metric

## The Smil Framework

Vaclav Smil's framework for understanding civilization rests on a simple and radical observation: modern civilization runs on four materials — cement, steel, plastics, and ammonia — and on the energy-intensive physical processes that transform them. Smil's framework is not about information, intelligence, or computation. It is about matter, energy, and the physical infrastructure that sustains human life. And it is this framework that most AI capability discourse has failed to engage with.

The Smil problem is the gap between the metrics we use to evaluate AI capability and the physical, material realities that determine what actually matters for civilization. A metric that measures only cognitive performance — reasoning, language, code generation — is measuring a subset of capability that is necessary but not sufficient for the kind of impact that transforms civilizations. The Smil problem is the recognition that we cannot measure AI capability correctly without measuring its relationship to the physical world — to the materials, the energy, the logistics, and the infrastructure that Smil identified as the substrate of modern life.

## The Cognitive Bias in AI Metrics

The cognitive bias in AI metrics is the tendency to evaluate AI systems primarily on cognitive tasks — tasks that involve information processing, reasoning, language, and decision-making. This bias is not accidental. Cognitive tasks are the easiest to formalize, the easiest to benchmark, and the easiest to optimize. They are also the tasks where AI systems have shown the most dramatic progress, which creates a feedback loop that reinforces the focus on cognitive metrics.

But cognitive tasks are a tiny fraction of what civilization depends on. The systems that keep civilization running — power grids, supply chains, agricultural systems, transportation networks, water treatment facilities, communication infrastructure — are not cognitive systems. They are physical systems, and their performance depends on physical capabilities that no cognitive metric captures.

The cognitive bias means that we are building and evaluating AI systems with a distorted picture of what matters. We are celebrating progress on tasks that are intellectually impressive but civilizationally marginal, while ignoring the physical capabilities that are civilizationally essential. This is not just a misallocation of research effort. It is a fundamental error in how we think about capability, and it will lead to systematic errors in how we predict the impact of AI on civilization.

## Toward a Physical Metric

A physical metric of AI capability would measure not just what a system can do in the cognitive domain, but what it can do in the physical domain — and how the two domains interact. This is not a call to replace cognitive metrics with physical metrics. It is a call to supplement them, to build a more complete picture of capability that includes both the cognitive and the physical, and to understand the relationship between them.

A physical metric would ask questions like: what fraction of the physical supply chain can this system optimize? What fraction of the physical infrastructure can this system monitor, maintain, or repair? What fraction of the material processes that sustain civilization can this system participate in, unassisted? These are not questions that can be answered by a benchmark score, but they are questions that matter — questions that determine whether AI capability is a force for civilizational transformation or a force for cognitive augmentation that leaves the physical world unchanged.

## The Metric We Actually Need

The metric we actually need is one that captures the relationship between cognitive capability and physical consequence. It would measure not just how smart a system is, but what that smartness enables it to do in the physical world — and how that capability changes the balance of power, resources, and outcomes between actors who do not trust each other.

This metric does not exist yet. Building it will require interdisciplinary work that bridges the cognitive sciences, the physical sciences, and the social sciences — work that is harder than building a benchmark but more important than any benchmark. The Smil problem is the recognition that until we build this metric, we are measuring AI capability with a tool that captures only half of what matters, and we are making decisions based on that incomplete measurement.

## A Note on Method

These notes are not a complete theory of AI capability measurement. They are an invitation — a call to look at the Smil problem, to recognize the cognitive bias in our metrics, and to start building the physical metrics that will give us a more honest picture of what these systems can and cannot do. The work ahead is long, and the metrics we build will be imperfect. But the commitment to building them honestly — and to measuring them honestly when they are imperfect — is the only foundation we accept.

---

The Smil problem is not just a measurement problem. It is a civilizational problem. The metrics we use to evaluate AI capability shape the decisions we make about what to build, what to fund, and what to trust. If those metrics are incomplete — if they measure only cognitive performance and ignore physical consequence — then the decisions they guide will be incomplete too. Building a correct metric is not just a research agenda. It is a civilizational necessity.
