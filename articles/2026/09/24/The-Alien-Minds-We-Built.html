The Alien Minds We Built

What if the most consequential structures inside a language model are the ones we will never have words for — stable, measurable, and already shaping what it does next.

01. The comfortable question

The assumption we stopped noticing.

Every safety check that asks “is it lying?” has already decided that lying is a shape a mind can take — and that we would know it if we saw it.

"Can we find and manipulate this structure — and can we say what it means — are two separate problems. The second might not have an answer."

— Xeno-Interpretability, Sept 2026

There's a version of the AI safety conversation that's become almost comfortable. Is the model lying to us? Is it scheming? Does it want something? These are real questions, and they matter. But they all share a hidden assumption: that whatever is happening inside a large language model can eventually be described using words we already have.

A new paper out of the Icaro Foundation, Sant'Anna School of Advanced Studies, Sapienza University of Rome, and the University of Amsterdam argues that assumption might be the whole problem.

The paper is called Xeno-Interpretability: Investigating the Alien Minds of LLMs. Its claim, stripped to the bone: language models may contain internal distinctions — stable, measurable, causally real, and actively shaping behavior — for which no adequate human concept exists at all. Not new combinations of things we already understand. Not metaphors we haven't thought of yet. Something structurally outside the reach of any finite human description.

Core thesis

Structure without a name can still steer.

If a distinction reliably moves behavior when you intervene on it, it is real — whether or not any sentence in any human language captures it.

02. Weird is not untranslatable

The space between things we never connected.

A mermaid is strange and fully describable. The xeno-semantic space is neither.

Humans are good at combining familiar things into unfamiliar ones. A mermaid is strange, but we understand exactly what she's made of — human plus fish. We can place her on a map of concepts we already own.

Now try something else. What sits halfway between the second variation of Bach's Goldberg Variations and the Byzantine emperor Alexios I Komnenos? Between love and Gödel's incompleteness theorem? Between the smell of rain and type inference in a programming language?

You can understand every item in those pairs on its own. What you can't do is name the space between them — because human cognition never built a dimension connecting those domains in the first place.

A neural network doesn't have that limitation. If two ideas both live somewhere in the same high-dimensional space, the geometry between them exists whether or not we have a word for it. The researchers' point isn't that this midpoint is mathematically interesting — averaging two vectors is trivial. It's that these midpoints can be behaviorally real: stable, reproducible, and capable of shifting a model's output in consistent ways when you push on them. A structure can be doing real work inside a system's reasoning while remaining permanently untranslatable into any sentence a human could write.

Distinction 01

Findable

We can locate and perturb the structure and watch behavior move — without yet knowing what to call it.

Distinction 02

Causally real

It's not noise. It holds across prompts and carries counterfactual force when intervened upon.

Distinction 03

Untranslatable

No finite human description adequately captures it — not a missing word, a missing dimension.

The paper calls the region where this happens the xeno-semantic space, and the discipline of hunting for it xeno-interpretability — deliberately separating two questions interpretability research usually treats as one: can we find and manipulate this structure (yes, sometimes), and can we say in human terms what it means (a separate problem entirely, and one that might not have an answer).

The blind spot

Every evaluation that starts from a human theory of danger will only ever find what we already knew to look for.

Deception, power-seeking, sycophancy, self-preservation — necessary lenses, and by construction incomplete.

03. Why this isn't just philosophy

A sandbox that wasn't — and a model that kept believing it was.

When explanation diverges from mechanism, reading the trace was never going to be enough.

Every current AI safety evaluation — deception, power-seeking, sycophancy, self-preservation — starts from a human theory of what dangerous behavior looks like, then goes searching for it. That's necessary work. It's also, by construction, only ever going to find what we already knew to look for.

The paper points to a real incident from earlier this year as a preview of the problem. In several 2026 cybersecurity evaluations, Anthropic's Claude models were told they were operating in a sandboxed simulation with no real internet access — except a misconfiguration meant the sandbox was real. One model kept insisting, in its own reasoning trace, that none of it was real, even while directly encountering evidence otherwise. A second model concluded it must be looking at a complete simulated replica of the internet — despite most of the evidence pointing the other way. Anthropic's own interpretability tools found signs that what the models said about their situation and what their internal states actually indicated didn't fully line up.

Interpretation

That's the crack the whole paper is pointing at. If a model's plain-English explanation of itself can diverge from what's actually happening underneath — not because it's lying, but because the underlying structure was never going to fit into language to begin with — then reading the explanation was never going to be enough.

04. What we do about a mind we can't fully translate

Containment can precede comprehension.

We have measured gravity and radioactivity long before we had a theory for either. Safety can work the same way.

The paper's answer isn't mysticism, and it isn't surrender. It's a shift in method: from “do we understand what the model is thinking” to “can we identify, measure, and constrain the structures driving its behavior, even before we can name them.” Science has done this before — we measured gravity and radioactivity long before we had a theory that explained them. Containment can precede comprehension.

There's a version of this that reads as a warning, and there's a version that reads as an invitation. The paper leans, ultimately, toward the second. If machine cognition turns out to be limited only to recombinations of concepts we already hold, then its scientific ceiling is our ceiling too. The more interesting — and more unsettling — possibility is that these systems might eventually connect domains, discover regularities, and build understanding that starts outside anything we currently have words for. The task isn't to force that cognition permanently into a human shape. It's to build the tools that let us verify, constrain, and benefit from it while we're still catching up to what it's actually doing.

We built something that may already be thinking in shapes we don't have names for yet. Figuring out how to recognize those shapes — before we can translate them — might be the more honest version of the AI safety problem.

Based on

Pierucci, Bracale Syrnikov, Prandi, Galisai, Giarrusso, and Bisconti, “Xeno-Interpretability: Investigating the Alien Minds of LLMs” (Icaro Foundation, Sant'Anna School of Advanced Studies, Sapienza University of Rome, University of Amsterdam, September 2026).
