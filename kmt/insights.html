Applied AI

From Pilot to P&L: Making Applied AI Stick

Most AI programs stall after the pilot. The ones that don't share an operating cadence—and a support model most firms skip.

The pattern is familiar. A promising proof of concept earns applause in a steering committee, then quietly dies between the demo and the day-to-day. The technology rarely fails; the operating model around it does. Nobody owns the workflow after go-live, the exception cases pile up, and within two quarters the pilot is a case study nobody cites.

In our engagements, the difference between a stalled pilot and a P&L line is unglamorous: instrument the real process before automating it, put a named owner on every handoff the agent touches, and contract for a support retainer that keeps the team on site until adoption is measured—not assumed. In one mid-market manufacturer, that discipline removed 31% of manual back-office effort inside two quarters. No frontier model required.

Post-Merger Integration

The First 100 Days: Integration Built on Evidence

Synergy targets fail in the gap between signing and operating. A roadmap closes that gap day by day.

Deals are approved on synergy slides and destroyed by integration reality. The failure usually isn't ambition—it's accounting. "Cost synergies" without owners, dates, and weekly tracking are hopes, not targets.

Our 100-day standard is deliberately narrow: a synergy register where every line has one accountable name, an AI-supported tracker that reconciles claims against actuals each week, and culture decisions made in the first fortnight—while the people the deal was actually about are still deciding whether to stay. Integrations run this way finish their synergy capture early; more importantly, they keep the customers and the key people they paid for.

Strategy & Market Entry

Market Entry Is an Evidence Problem

Investment-grade research beats instinct. How we screen markets before our clients commit capital.

Every board has a market-entry story it tells itself. The evidence usually says something quieter: the addressable market is smaller than the deck claims, the price points are lower, or the competitor you dismissed is winning on service, not scale.

We run fixed-fee screens built bottom-up—named competitors, auditable demand data, real price points—and we deliver the answer the evidence supports, including "don't enter." For a European industrial group weighing three markets last year, the screen ranked them clearly: one to enter now, one to enter through partnership, and one to leave alone. The discipline of the no is worth as much as the yes.

Operating Model

What 20–30% Efficiency Actually Requires

Process data, honest benchmarking, and the discipline to redesign roles—not just tools.

Efficiency programs promise 20–30% because the waste is real. What the promise omits is that capturing it means changing what specific people do on Monday morning. Tools can be procured; accountability has to be designed.

We baseline core processes with measured cycle times and rework loops, benchmark against operators who actually publish numbers, then rebuild spans, layers, and roles so accountability lands somewhere specific. A recent professional-services program delivered a 22% efficiency gain—not the top of the range, but real, banked, and still compounding because the operating rhythm survived the engagement team's departure.
